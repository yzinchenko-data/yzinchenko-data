Dreams of thinking machines

In 1950, the British mathematician Alan Turing asked a deceptively simple question: can machines think? Rather than getting lost in philosophy, he proposed a practical test. If a person chatting through a keyboard could not reliably tell a machine from a human, the machine, he argued, deserved to be called intelligent.

Six years later, a small group of researchers gathered at Dartmouth College and coined the term “artificial intelligence”. They were remarkably optimistic, predicting that a single summer of work could make significant progress. That optimism turned out to be premature. Over the following decades, the field went through cycles of hype and disappointment, including long periods of reduced funding that became known as “AI winters”.

Progress, however, never fully stopped. In 1997, IBM’s Deep Blue defeated the world chess champion Garry Kasparov. Yet Deep Blue relied on brute-force search and hand-crafted rules written by experts. It could not learn anything outside chess, and it certainly could not hold a conversation.

“I propose to consider the question, ‘Can machines think?’”Alan Turing, Computing Machinery and Intelligence, 1950

The deep learning revolution

The real turning point came in 2012. A neural network called AlexNet, trained on ordinary graphics cards, dramatically outperformed every rival in an image recognition contest. Researchers realised that neural networks, an old idea that had long been dismissed, could become astonishingly capable if they were given enough data and computing power.

In 2016, DeepMind’s AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol, one of the strongest Go players in history, a feat that experts had expected to take another decade. A year later, eight Google researchers published a paper with a modest title, “Attention Is All You Need”. It introduced the Transformer, an architecture that processes words in parallel and learns which parts of a text should pay attention to which others. Almost every modern language model, including Claude, is built on this design.

What followed is often called the scaling era. Researchers noticed that model performance improved predictably as they increased three ingredients: the size of the model, the amount of training data and the amount of compute. These relationships, known as scaling laws, turned AI progress into something closer to an engineering problem, and a very expensive one. A different kind of AI company

In 2021, a group of seven researchers left OpenAI to start a new company. Among them were the siblings Dario Amodei, who had led research on large language models such as GPT-2 and GPT-3, and Daniela Amodei, who had overseen safety and policy work. They named the company Anthropic, a word that means “relating to human beings”.

The founders shared a conviction that was unusual at the time. They believed that AI systems would soon become extraordinarily powerful, and that this power could bring enormous benefits but also serious risks. Instead of staying on the sidelines, they decided to build frontier models themselves, so that they could study them up close and show that safety and capability do not have to be a trade-off.

Anthropic was set up as a public benefit corporation, a legal structure that obliges the company to consider its impact on society, not only on shareholders. It later created the Long-Term Benefit Trust, an independent body that gradually gains the power to elect members of the company’s board. The company’s headquarters are in San Francisco.

Teaching a model values

How do you teach a machine to be helpful without being harmful? The standard method, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), relies on thousands of people rating the model’s answers. In December 2022, Anthropic published an alternative called Constitutional AI. The model is given a written set of principles, a “constitution”, and learns to critique and revise its own responses according to them. The principles drew on sources as varied as the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

In September 2023, the company introduced its Responsible Scaling Policy. Inspired by the biosafety levels used in laboratories that handle dangerous pathogens, the policy defines AI Safety Levels, or ASL. The more capable a model becomes, the stricter the safeguards it requires before it can be trained further or released. When Claude Opus 4 launched in May 2025, it was the first model deployed with the stronger ASL-3 protections, as a precaution.

Anthropic also invests heavily in interpretability, the science of looking inside a neural network to understand how it reaches its conclusions. In 2024, its researchers identified millions of “features” inside Claude: patterns of activity that correspond to concepts. In a famous experiment they amplified one of them and created “Golden Gate Claude”, a version of the model that was obsessed with the San Francisco bridge and mentioned it in almost every answer.

Meet Claude

The launch of ChatGPT in November 2022 turned AI into a mass-market product almost overnight. Anthropic’s own assistant, Claude, became publicly available in March 2023. Its name is widely seen as a nod to Claude Shannon, the father of information theory. Claude 2 followed in July and could read about 75,000 words at once, roughly the length of a novel.

In March 2024, the Claude 3 family introduced a naming scheme borrowed from poetry. Each name describes a size, from the short Japanese poem to the great musical work:

Small · fastHaiku

A three-line poem. The quickest and cheapest model.

BalancedSonnet

A fourteen-line poem. The everyday workhorse.

LargestOpus

A major work. The most capable model.

Each generation also brought a larger context window, the amount of text a model can consider at one time. It is measured in tokens, small pieces of text that are usually shorter than a word. In the English language, 1,000 tokens are roughly 750 words.

    From chatbot to coworker

    By 2024, the question was no longer whether a model could answer questions, but whether it could get things done. In October 2024, Anthropic introduced “computer use”: Claude could look at a screen, move the cursor, click buttons and type, much as a person would. A month later came the Model Context Protocol (MCP), an open standard that lets AI assistants connect to tools and data sources in a uniform way. It was quickly adopted across the industry, including by competitors.

    In February 2025, the company released Claude Code, a tool that lets developers delegate programming tasks to Claude directly from the terminal. Instead of suggesting a line here and there, the model can read an entire codebase, run tests and fix its own mistakes. Programming soon became one of Claude’s most important use cases.

    A benchmark called SWE-bench Verified became a popular way to track this progress. It checks whether a model can resolve real problems taken from open-source projects on GitHub. In about a year, Claude’s score rose from under half to more than eighty percent.

    Chapter 072023 – 20253 min read

    The economics of the frontier

    Building frontier models requires staggering amounts of money. Training runs consume the power of vast data centres full of specialised chips, and the cost of each generation has grown steadily. To pay for this, Anthropic formed partnerships with cloud giants. Amazon committed a total of $8 billion and became the company’s primary training partner, while Google also invested billions of dollars and supplies its TPU chips.

    Investors have been betting heavily on the company’s growth. Its valuation climbed from about $4 billion in 2023 to $183 billion in September 2025. Revenue grew just as fast: the company’s annualised revenue rose from roughly $1 billion at the start of 2025 to more than $5 billion by August, driven by businesses and developers using Claude through the API.

    Chapter 08Today2 min read

    The road ahead

    In October 2024, Dario Amodei published a long essay titled “Machines of Loving Grace”. In it he argued that powerful AI could compress decades of scientific progress into a few years, helping to cure diseases, lift billions of people out of poverty and strengthen democracy. He was equally candid about the risks: misuse, loss of control and the concentration of power.

    This tension between promise and peril defines the company. Critics point out that a lab worried about the dangers of AI is also racing to build it. Anthropic’s answer is that someone will build these systems anyway, and it is better if safety-focused developers are at the frontier. Whether that bet pays off is one of the most consequential questions of our time.

    Either way, the story is still being written. And now you can read it in English.
